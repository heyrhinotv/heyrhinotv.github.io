one energy drink.

What one can does to your stomach, brain, heart and sleep, stop by stop. And who profits.

it's not energy. it's a loan.

Caffeine doesn't make energy. It borrows it from later, and you pay it back with interest. Call it the energy loan.

1stomach0–10 min

a whole day of sugar.

A typical can has about 40 g of sugar ≈ 10 teaspoons. The American Heart Association's cap on added sugar is about 9 tsp a day for men and 6 for women. One can beats it before dinner.

Caffeine moves fast: some is absorbed right in your stomach, the rest in your small intestine, and about 99% is in your blood within ~45 minutes. Then it walks straight through the wall that guards your brain.

1 can > a whole day of sugar.

2brain + heart15–60 min

it hides tired. it doesn't add energy.

All day your brain piles up adenosine, a mail carrier with one message: "you're tired." Caffeine sits in its docks without delivering the message, so the messages pile up in the lobby. Peak caffeine: about 1 hour in.

Your heart: in a small Mayo Clinic study (25 young adults, one 16-oz can), blood pressure rose ~6% vs ~1% with a placebo, and the stress hormone norepinephrine rose more than twice as much.

still hour one: the sugar rush is a myth.

31 studies, almost 1,300 adults: sugar didn't lift mood, and within an hour people felt more tired and less alert. The "energy blend" (taurine, ginseng, guarana) isn't backed by solid controlled trials, and extra B vitamins mostly leave in your pee. B2 is why it looks so yellow.

3bedtime5 hrs

half is still in you.

Your liver clears it (one enzyme does over 90% of the first step). Caffeine's half-life is about 3–6 hours for many adults. Call it 5. With a 200 mg can:

3 pm · 200 mg→8 pm · ~100→1 am · ~50

In a sleep-lab study, 400 mg six hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour, and people didn't notice. When it clears, the stored-up tired walks in at once: the crash, with interest.

Your clock isn't my clock: smokers clear it faster (~6 h → 3.5 h), estrogen birth-control pills slow it by more than half, and late in pregnancy it can stretch to ~10.5 h.

4the billwho profits

the part that wakes you up costs less than a penny.

Bulk caffeine trades for about $14 a kilogram, so 200 mg costs about $0.003. You paid a few bucks.

The biggest energy drink company on the U.S. stock market sold about $8.3 billion of drinks last year, kept about 56¢ of every $1 after making and shipping them, then spent about $2.2 billion running the company: marketing, sponsorships, athletes, people.

Not a scam, a business. You're paying for the feeling, not the caffeine. know what you're paying for.

rhino's rule.

No caffeine within 6 hours of bed, and treat that as the bare minimum. The researchers behind the sleep study said caffeine should generally be avoided after 5 p.m.

Need energy later than that? You're already in debt. Only sleep pays it off.

the only energy that doesn't come with interest.

read the label.

16-oz energy drinks range from 54 to 328 mg of caffeine (FDA). About 400 mg a day is usually fine for healthy adults; seizures can show up with ~1,200 mg taken fast.

ER visits involving energy drinks doubled to almost 21,000 a year (2007–2011); about 4 in 10 also involved other drugs or alcohol. Experts advise against energy drinks for kids and teens.
